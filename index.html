Part One

Rhetorical Situation

The Artist

Rodarius Marcell Green, known as Rod Wave, born on August 27, 1998, in St. Petersburg, Florida. His upbringing on the south side of the city, one of Florida's most economically divided areas, was defined by instability: his parents separated when he was only in elementary school, and his father then went to prison for six years, which left the family to struggle financially.

Forced to navigate poverty along with absence of a father figure, Rod turned to the streets as a teenager, accumulating juvenile record entries and a persistent fear that he'd never escape the cycle. He started singing in 5th grade but he didn't pursue music seriously until high school. He graduated from Lakewood High School in 2017 and released his debut mixtape that same year.

By the time SoulFly arrived in 2021, Rod Wave was 22 years old, a self-made artist who went from street corners to national television in under 4 years, driven entirely by emotional honesty and a great voice that sounds like it carries the weight of every experience he's ever lived.

"I feel like before music, I didn't really know what I wanted to do… Now, I'm making music and actually living life and being the one that people depend on. It gave me a reason to wake up."
— Rod Wave, Billboard 2020

His stage name itself shows his aspiration, he intended to make waves in the music industry, and by 2021, he did. His first two albums had peaked at #10 and #2 on the Billboard 200. SoulFly would be the album where he finally reached the top

Genre & Tone

Rod Wave is widely considered as a pioneer of trap-soul ,a interesting genre that combines the moody 808 driven production of trap with the vulnerability, melody, and emotional depth of soul and R&B. Unlike a lot of rappers his age who focus on flexing and confidence, Rod Wave stands out because he’s really open about his emotions and isn’t afraid to be vulnerable.

The tone of SoulFly is a lot of bittersweet endurance. It's not a victory lap and it's not a sob story, it's the complicated feeling of making it in life while still carrying the memories of where you came from. Pride, grief, love, anxiety, and gratitude all influence the record in big ways.

World Context (2020–2021)

The world that received SoulFly in March 2021 was emerging, bruised, from one of the most difficult and abnormal periods in modern American history. The COVID-19 pandemic had halted live music, rapidly grew streaming, and left millions of Americans feeling isolated and searching for connection through art.

2020 had also been a year of heightened national reckoning with racial injustice, the murders of George Floyd, Breonna Taylor, and others sparked a widespread movement that gave new urgency to Black artists noting the psychic costs of growing up in underprivileged communities. Rod Wave's portrayal of that world, the missed calls that mean bad news, the fear every time his phone rings, and the constant threat of losing people to the streets.

Core Message

The title says it all. SoulFly is about liberation, the kind that only comes after surviving deep emotional pain. The album highlights the journey from survival to transcendence, from street life to a life worth living, and ultimately toward the only true rest Rod Wave imagines: peace after death. It's an album about wanting to be remembered for being real, not just achieving success.

🎵

Previous Work

SoulFly followed Pray 4 Love (2020), which peaked at #2 and included the hit song, "Rags2Riches." This third album represented Rod Wave's most ambitious and fully realized statement to date.

🌍

Pandemic Release

Released during the final months of worldwide COVID pandemic lockdowns, SoulFly tapped into a national mood of grief, resilience, and searching for meaning, it reached listeners who were hungry for genuine emotional connection.

📻

TikTok & Streaming Era

"Heart on Ice" (2019) had gone viral on TikTok, introducing Rod Wave to millions of young listeners. By 2021, his audience was dedicated and enormous, SoulFly racked up 189.2 million on demand streams in its first week.

Part Two

Lyrical Analysis

05

Tombstone

Track 5 · 2:40 · The Breakout Single

Overview & Setup

"Tombstone" is built on a skeletal 808 bass pattern that creates a feeling of emptiness, like standing in a vast, open field with nowhere to hide. That choice is intentional: Rod Wave is confronting mortality directly, and the production refuses to let the listener feel comfortable.

The song opens with Rod facing the hardest parts of life in the streets: the ever present fear something bad happening, the pressure of providing for family, and the exhaustion of constantly looking over his shoulder.

"My phone say seven missed calls, I know it's bad news / This life had left me so scarred / I keep my gun in my drawers, duckin' the sad news"

Verse Development

The first verse establishes the stakes: survival in an environment where violence is a consistent threat and bad news arrives without warning. Rod Wave builds tension with specific, grounded imagery rather than abstractions, missed calls, hiding a weapon, running from authority. These aren't metaphors; they are the literal texture of his daily life.

The second verse shifts into the complicated dynamics of relationships under pressure, a partner who wants connection, a man whose focus must stay on securing his family's future. He acknowledges the tension without apologizing for it, showing the cost that street life and hustle exact on intimacy.

The Chorus

The chorus is where the song really starts to hit you. Over a gospel choir, Rod Wave says what feels like his own final message:

"On my tombstone when they bury me / By the river, they will carry me / Finally, I'll be restin' in peace"

The word finally is doing an enormous amount of work here. It frames death not as tragedy but as relief, the ultimate release from a life lived under constant pressure. The gospel choir transforms what could be a morbid sentiment into something approaching spiritual longing. Rod Wave doesn't want to die; he wants the peace that death represents but that life has never given him.

The Outro, A Shift in Register

The outro pulls back the curtain further, Rod directly addresses posthumous fame, the phenomenon of artists being celebrated only after death. His concern isn't about legacy for legacy's sake; it's a protest against a culture that refuses to appreciate people while they're alive.

"Make sure they write the truest in the motherf***in' game / On my tombstone when they bury me"

Literary Devices

Extended Metaphor — The Tombstone

The tombstone functions throughout the song as a metaphor for legacy, identity, and the desire to be known truthfully. Rod Wave isn't writing about a literal tombstone; he's writing about how a person is remembered and reduced to a few words after a lifetime of complexity. By centering this image, he challenges us to consider whether we truly see the people around us before it's too late.

Juxtaposition — Street Life vs. Spiritual Peace

The production pairs violent, street based imagery (guns, missed calls, running from authorities) with explicitly spiritual and gospel elements (the choir, the river imagery of baptism, the language of rest and peace). This juxtaposition is the heart of the song's meaning: Rod Wave exists simultaneously in a world of danger and a world of spiritual longing. The two are inseparable in his experience.

Allusion — "By the River"

The image of being "carried by the river" alludes to both African American spiritual traditions (rivers as pathways to freedom, as in the Underground Railroad) and Christian baptism imagery. It places Rod Wave's personal story within a much larger historical and spiritual context of Black Americans seeking freedom and peace.

Album Contribution

"Tombstone" is the philosophical spine of SoulFly. While other songs address specific topics (relationships, money, trauma), this track asks the album's deepest question: what does any of it mean if you don't survive long enough to know peace? It's the song that gives the album its title its fullest meaning, the soul, finally, free to fly.

Critically, it was performed on The Tonight Show with Jimmy Fallon on April 8, 2021, Rod Wave's national TV debut, introducing this deeply personal vision to a mainstream audience.

08

Street Runner

Track 8 · 4:12 · Lead Single

Overview & Setup

"Street Runner" is the album's lead single, the first song released ahead of SoulFly ,and it opens with a sampled interpolation from Ruth B.'s "Mixed Signals," a melancholy indie folk track about romantic ambiguity. That choice immediately signals Rod Wave's emotional range: this is a song about love that has become complicated by a lifestyle that leaves little room for it.

At 4:12, it's one of the album's longer tracks, and it earns that runtime through a slow, patient construction of emotional complexity. Where "Tombstone" is about surviving the streets, "Street Runner" is about what the streets cost in terms of the relationships you most want to hold onto.

"These mixed signals, mixed signals, they're killin' me / I don't know what you want, but I know what I need"

Verse Development

The opening verse places the listener in the middle of a fractured relationship where physical distance mirrors emotional distance. Rod Wave is constantly moving from city to city, stage to stage, and the woman he loves is left behind, uncertain of her place in a life that has no room to slow down.

The second verse escalates the tension. Rod Wave shifts from simply describing the situation to interrogating his own role in it. His inability to fully give himself to a relationship isn't framed as selfishness but as a symptom of the environment he came from, where trusting people fully could get you hurt, and where keeping your guard up was a survival skill that never quite switches off.

The Chorus

The chorus circles back to the sampled theme of mixed signals, but with Rod Wave's characteristic twist, he acknowledges the mixed signals aren't just coming from his partner. He's sending them too. He wants closeness but can't stop running. The chorus is a confession as much as a complaint:

"I don't know what you want, but I know what I need / Out here in these streets, I'm a street runner / Streets keep callin' me"

Literary Devices

Personification — The Streets

"The streets keep calling me" is a form of personification that transforms an abstract environment into an active, seductive force. The streets aren't just a place Rod Wave came from; they're something that claims him, calls to him, competes with the people he loves for his attention and loyalty. This makes the conflict feel tragic rather than simply irresponsible.

Intertextual Sampling — Ruth B.

The deliberate use of Ruth B.'s "Mixed Signals" as the song's sonic foundation is itself a literary device, intertextuality. By borrowing the emotional atmosphere of a soft, indie folk track about romantic confusion, Rod Wave places his trap soul song in conversation with a completely different genre and audience. It signals that the pain of mixed signals is universal, regardless of genre, background, or circumstance. It also adds layers of meaning for listeners who recognize the source material.

Anaphora — Repetition of "These Mixed Signals"

The repetition of the phrase "mixed signals" throughout the song functions as anaphora, a rhetorical device that builds emotional weight through structured repetition. Each return to the phrase lands differently as the song's context deepens, moving from frustration to self awareness to something approaching grief.

Album Contribution

As the lead single and the album's midpoint, "Street Runner" is the emotional anchor of SoulFly. It's the song that most directly addresses the cost of success, not in material terms but in relational ones. Positioned at track 8 of 19, it functions as a hinge point: everything before it builds toward this moment of romantic reckoning, and everything after it examines what it means to keep moving even when you've been emotionally opened up. It's the album's most commercially successful track and its most emotionally honest one simultaneously.

Part Three

Tracklist Line of Reasoning

Movement I — Tracks 1–4
Grounding in Pain
SoulFly Gone Till November Blame on You Don't Forget

The album opens with the title track, which functions as a thesis statement: Rod Wave declares that despite his commercial success, the pain never fully goes away, but he's made peace with that. "Gone Till November" immediately shifts to the cost of the touring life on personal relationships. "Blame on You" digs into accountability and the difficulty of trusting people who've let you down. "Don't Forget" is a direct plea to the culture not to lose the roots of what makes music meaningful. These four tracks plant the album firmly in Rod Wave's lived reality before anything else is explored.

Movement II — Tracks 5–7
Reckoning with Mortality and Success
Tombstone All I Got Richer (ft. Polo G)

"Tombstone" arrives at track 5, the album's philosophical core. Its placement here, before the big commercial single, is intentional. Rod Wave wants you to understand the stakes before you hear the hook. "All I Got" follows as a more grounded reflection on having very little before music arrived. Then "Richer", the album's only feature on the standard version, celebrates the come up with Polo G, but even this celebration is shadowed by the awareness of how fragile success is. This three track sequence is the album's darkest and most introspective pocket.

Movement III — Tracks 8–12
Love, Flex, and the World Outside
Street Runner Pills & Billz How the Game Go Shock da World What's Love??

The album's midsection represents a tonal shift, not a lightening exactly, but a widening of perspective. "Street Runner" opens this section with relational pain; "Pills & Billz" and "How the Game Go" bring more street level observation. "Shock da World" is one of the album's more defiant, upward looking tracks. "What's Love??" adds complexity by questioning even the relationships that feel most stable. Rod Wave uses this stretch to show all the competing demands on his attention and energy.

Movement IV — Tracks 13–19
Resolution and Release
OMDB Invisible Scar Calling Sneaky Links Believe Me Moving On Changing

“OMDB,” which samples Drake’s “Over My Dead Body,” is a defensive, guarded track where Rod Wave shuts out the noise of critics and doubters. From there, the album moves steadily toward emotional resolution. “Invisible Scar” and “Calling” revisit trauma with more acceptance. “Sneaky Links” adds a rare moment of levity. “Believe Me” and “Moving On” push toward the future in exactly the way their titles suggest. The closer, “Changing,” completes the arc, with Rod Wave acknowledging that he is not the same person he once was and that growth, while painful, is the only real response to everything that came before. It serves as a gentle, intentional landing after an emotionally turbulent flight.

Key Artistic Choices

01
Title Track Opens the Album

Placing “SoulFly” as the opening track rather than the closer signals the album’s central idea from the start. The image of a soul in flight, searching for freedom, is introduced first so everything that follows can expand on it. In that sense, each track becomes evidence that builds on the statement made at the beginning.

02
"Tombstone" Before the Single

The album’s deepest, most spiritually heavy track comes right before its most commercially appealing one. Rod Wave does not want you to just stream your favorite song and move on. He wants you to sit with mortality before you reach love and street life. The sequencing pushes you toward depth before surface.

03
One Feature (Standard Edition)

With only Polo G featured on the standard 19 track album, Rod Wave keeps the project intentionally personal. Guest verses naturally introduce other voices and perspectives, so limiting features to just one track helps the album feel like a single, unified confession rather than a collaborative showcase.

04
"Changing" as the Closer

Ending on “Changing,” a soft, reflective track about personal growth, is a statement of humility. For an artist who could have closed with a triumphant flex, choosing quiet transformation instead sends a clear message: the real victory is becoming someone better, not just someone bigger.

Reception at Release

SoulFly debuted at number one on the US Billboard 200 chart in April 2021, marking Rod Wave’s first number one album and achieving the largest streaming week for any R&B/hip hop album at that point in the year. Billboard’s Jason Lipshutz called it Rod Wave’s “most authoritative project to date.” The Fader described it as “something like an intense conversation,” where Wave makes “his heart’s concerns sound universal.”"

Critical reception was largely positive, with reviewers praising Rod Wave’s vocals and lyrical sincerity. Some critics pointed to the album’s length and consistent tone as potential drawbacks for casual listeners, but his fanbase strongly disagreed, connecting deeply with its unfiltered emotional honesty.

Lasting Impact on the Genre

SoulFly cemented Rod Wave’s role as a defining architect of trap soul, a genre that has since grown in influence. His approach of pairing trap production with R&B vulnerability and confessional lyricism has been widely imitated, but rarely replicated with the same authenticity. The album showed that commercial success and emotional depth are not opposites in hip hop. You can have both.

The album also demonstrated how TikTok era virality could be channeled into full album level artistry. Rod Wave was not just a singles artist. He was building entire emotional worlds that reward full listens. In a streaming era that often prioritizes individual tracks, SoulFly made a strong case for the album as a complete artistic statement.

Critical Legacy Over Time

SoulFly was ranked the seventh best album of 2021 by The Atlantic, a notable recognition from a publication not typically centered on hip hop. AllMusic’s Andy Kellman praised the sincerity that has become Rod Wave’s signature. Apple Music also named it the most pre-saved album on the platform for two consecutive weeks before its release.

In the years since, SoulFly has come to be seen as the moment Rod Wave moved from a promising voice to a generational one. It remains his most defining artistic statement, and it is the album most often cited when people explain why Rod Wave matters.

Why This Album

SoulFly resonates because it is an album about surviving without pretending that survival is enough. Rod Wave refuses the false choice between celebrating success or drowning in trauma. Instead, he holds both at the same time, which feels like a more honest reflection of what it means to come from very little and still make it somewhere.

In an era when vulnerability in hip hop is sometimes performed rather than truly felt, Rod Wave’s approach sounds clearly lived in. Every missed call that could be bad news, every relationship strained by distance, every moment of exhausted longing for peace, all of it carries the weight of real experience. That kind of authenticity is rare, and that is why SoulFly endures.

What makes SoulFly stay with you is not any single moment, but the overall weight of 19 tracks that never pretend life is simpler than it is. Rod Wave does not resolve his pain by the end of the album. He just decides to keep moving anyway. That choice, more than any chart position, is what makes this record matter.

Part Five

Track Ratings

8.6

Overall Album Rating

SoulFly is a really consistent, emotional album built around one of the most recognizable voices in modern hip hop. That consistency is kind of what makes it great, even if some people might find it a little repetitive at times. But if you connect with Rod Wave’s emotional style, it hits almost perfectly. Overall, it stands out as one of the defining albums of its time.
